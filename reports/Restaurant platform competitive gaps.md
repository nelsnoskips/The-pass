# Win the dining room competitors left behind

**The Pass's best opening is the full-service independent that wants an owned, neutral reservation and guest system, sold flat and month to month, with the website, marketing, reviews and ads agents on top.** Every well-funded all-in-one is built for off-premises orders. Owner.com ($2.3B valuation, more than $100M ARR) and Open (Sequoia-backed) start from website plus commission-free ordering. The three reservation leaders are now owned by companies with other businesses to protect: Booking Holdings owns OpenTable, American Express owns Resy and Tock, and DoorDash owns SevenRooms. Operators have concrete reasons to switch right now. OpenTable's March 2026 contract made it the required "system of record" with heavy early-termination liability. Tock's app is being shut down as it folds into Resy. The FTC and seven states sued Toast on October 29, 2026 over hidden fees and cancellation traps. Uber's pending $2.3B purchase of ezCater has caterers expecting higher commissions. The Pass already has most of what this wedge needs: a reservations engine, host stand, guest list with consent records, import/export, Square visit sync, and live Paid Ads, Reservations, Website and Reviews agents. Its advantage is data no POS-bound or ordering-first rival holds: who booked, who sat, when, and how often. The recommended go-to-market is a free "leak audit" in the style of Owner's Grader, plus switch kits timed to those triggers, selling a reservations-led core plan near the $129–$199 POS-native price band, then upselling a flat full-suite plan in the $499–$799 range. The full suite is pitched against stacked tool bills that run roughly $1,500–$2,500 a month. The US base is about **412,500 independent restaurants**, shrinking 2.3% a year. That puts a flat-fee dollar TAM at roughly $2.5–5B ARR. Owner's roughly 23x ARR valuation, along with $400M–$2.3B strategic exits, shows that both investors and acquirers pay for this category.

## Three parent companies now own the reservation book

### Who leads and how they price

Reservations is a three-way contest among companies that each have another business to protect. OpenTable "controls 60,000 restaurants" and Resy "will reach 25,000 venues after integrating Tock" ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/doordash-buys-12b-reservation-platform-to-battle-opentable/502986)). **DoorDash completed its $1.2B all-cash purchase of SevenRooms** ([DoorDash 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1792789/000162828025022396/q12025dashex991-pressrelea.htm)). On September 30, 2026, DoorDash folded SevenRooms into **DashOS**. DashOS combines marketplace, direct ordering, reservations, loyalty, guest management, marketing and SevenRooms Voice AI on one platform, and its marketing tools use "blinded, aggregated DoorDash Marketplace insights." DoorDash has published no pricing for it, and its omnichannel loyalty does not arrive until 2027 ([DoorDash IR](https://ir.doordash.com/news/news-details/2026/DoorDash-Introduces-DashOS-One-Platform-to-Help-Restaurants-Grow/default.aspx)). Amex bought Tock from Squarespace for $400M in 2024 ([Resy newsroom](https://blog.resy.com/newsroom/to-enhance-dining-platform-american-express-enters-agreement-to-acquire-tock-from-squarespace-also-agrees-to-acquire-rooam/)). It is now folding Tock into Resy and retiring the Tock app and exploretock.com ([The Points Guy](https://thepointsguy.com/news/amex-resy-credit-tock-restaurants/?utm_section=ts)). Every Tock venue therefore faces a forced re-platforming.

Each leader is good at something real. OpenTable brings the most diners. SevenRooms has the deepest guest CRM and now bundles AI phone answering. Resy has Amex card credits that steer premium diners to its venues. All three now pay diners with someone else's money: DoorDash gives **$10–12 in credit per booking** and charges restaurants "no cover fees for reservations made on the DoorDash app ever" ([Food On Demand](https://foodondemand.com/05122026/doordash-ramps-up-reservations-with-largest-expansion-yet/)). ### What operators complain about

The sharpest new grievance is contractual. **OpenTable's March 2026 agreement** works like this:

| Term | What it says |
|---|---|
| Cancelling mid-term, initial term | Restaurant owes 100% of remaining subscription fees plus 100% of remaining cover fees |
| Cancelling mid-term, renewal term | 50% of remaining subscription plus 100% of cover fees |
| Fees after the initial term | OpenTable may modify them "in its sole discretion" ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/technology/articles/why-restaurants-anxious-opentable-terms-105500851.html)) |
| "System of record" | OpenTable must be the restaurant's primary system for reservations, table and guest management |
| Inventory | All tables must be made available on the OpenTable marketplace ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/opentable-will-require-restaurants-make-it-their-primary-reservations-system)) |

A Seattle chamber leader asked the Washington AG to review the terms, and the antitrust division agreed to examine whether they "may constitute an anticompetitive practice" ([Washington State Standard](https://washingtonstatestandard.com/2026/04/15/opentables-new-rules-have-a-seattle-business-leader-calling-foul/)).

Switching also fails in practice. When Union Square Cafe moved from Resy to SevenRooms, "the restaurant's notes on some guests weren't showing up in the system." USHG later moved again, from DoorDash-owned SevenRooms to OpenTable ([Caper](https://caper.media/p/union-square-hospitality-group-reservation-move-sevenrooms-opentable)).

One common belief does not hold up: measured no-show rates are low. The resOS index covers 3.77M reservations and puts the average at **2.33%**: 0.80% with card or deposit protection versus 2.47% without ([resOS](https://resos.com/restaurant-no-show-rate/)). Bloom Intelligence reports 3.7% no-shows plus 15% cancellations ([Bloom Intelligence](https://bloomintelligence.com/blog/restaurant-no-shows/)). resOS skews toward European venues. Either way, putting deposits on every booking would mostly add friction. Targeted deposits on prime Friday and Saturday slots and large parties match the actual risk.

Resale laws are spreading. New York requires a written agreement before third parties list reservations, with fines of $1,000 per day ([Holland & Knight](https://hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/02/new-york-curbs-scalping-of-restaurant-reservations)). Florida, Hawaii and Nevada have similar laws ([SC Daily Gazette](https://scdailygazette.com/2025/06/27/black-market-dining-reservations-sell-for-thousands-states-want-to-stop-that/)). California's AB 1640 passed the Assembly; its final status is unverified ([LegiScan](https://legiscan.com/CA/bill/AB1640/2172)).

### How The Pass capitalizes

The Pass has already built most of what this segment asks for, according to `docs/AGENTIC-PLATFORM.md` and `docs/FRICTION.md`:

- a reservations engine that refuses overlapping holds whatever the booking source
- one live booking per phone per service, with rate limits
- one-tap change or cancel
- a notify list for openings
- an import tool with a dry run
- one-click export of guests and consent history
- a host stand
- a Reservations Agent that fills quiet shifts by SMS

Four things are missing:

| Gap | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Live Stripe deposits at processing cost | The pitch is $0 platform cut vs OpenTable's 2% |
| Host-stand offline mode | Hosts need it when the connection drops |
| Reserve with Google certification | The native "Reserve a table" button needs certified partner status and an active booking API connection ([Google Actions Center](https://developers.google.com/actions-center/verticals/reservations/e2e/overview)). Without it, The Pass can only show a plain link. |
| AI phone answering for bookings | DashOS ships it, so it is now parity |

The import tool needs one specific upgrade. It must carry over **guest notes, tags, allergies and visit history**, which is exactly what visibly failed at Union Square Cafe. A hosted "nothing lost" verification report would turn the riskiest moment of a switch into the selling moment.

OpenTable's system-of-record clause also creates a design constraint. A restaurant still under contract likely cannot make The Pass its primary system without breaching the agreement. The Pass therefore needs a defined **coexistence mode** that takes only direct bookings (website, Google, Instagram) until the contract ends, subject to legal review of OpenTable's terms. This also partly counters the one advantage The Pass cannot match, a diner network. The restaurant can measure how many network covers it actually needs before it leaves.

### What operators complain about

The documented complaints fall into five groups:

1. **Lock-in and billing after cancellation.** Popmenu customers report being billed after cancelling in writing and being required to pay out the rest of a **24-month** contract. Popmenu's BBB replies say there is no early termination right "absent Popmenu's breach" ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/us/ga/atlanta/profile/marketing-software/popmenu-0443-28164736/complaints)).
2. **Fees stacked on "commission-free."** Processing, subscription, delivery and diner fees still apply ([Beyond Menu](https://get.beyondmenu.com/blog/commission-free-online-ordering-restaurants)).
3. **Fees imposed on guests without consent.** Toast's 2023 $0.99 guest fee sparked a "mom-and-pop restaurant revolt" and was reversed within days ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/toast-remove-99-cent-fee-after-widespread-backlash)).
4. **Lost sales after migrating.** One Owner reviewer reported online sales falling at least $6,000 in the first month after the switch ([Capterra](https://capterra.com/p/10002488/Owner/)).
5. **Operational menu sync.** Separate tablets for each delivery app, re-keying orders into the POS, and **86'd items still selling online** ([Richmenu](https://richmenu.io/restaurant-online-ordering/)).

### How The Pass capitalizes

The Pass's pickup ordering is built: site menu, server-side pricing, tax, tips, Stripe, kitchen board, pause and sold-out. Catering orders with notice, headcount and minimums also work. But The Pass is missing what every ordering competitor treats as table stakes:

- **item modifiers**
- **POS hand-off** to Toast and Square
- **white-label delivery** through DoorDash Drive or Uber Direct
- **Google ordering links**

Ordering should not be the wedge. Owner and Open have years and hundreds of millions of dollars of head start there.

Two adjacent moves do fit what The Pass has built. The first is **commission-free catering with quotes, Stripe deposits and invoices**, marketed as "keep your ezCater regulars direct." Because the guest list already holds catering contacts, a "reorder your last catering order" agent proposal is a natural follow-on. The second is a **trust pricing page** that states, in writing, no guest fees, no per-order fees, self-serve cancellation and an all-in cost calculator. That position is easy to see against Owner's 5% guest fee, Popmenu's lock-in and ChowNow's 5% catering cut.

The Website Agent, which drafts a described change, previews it and publishes on approval, answers the slow-edit problem common to site vendors. That pain is plausible but not directly sourced, so it needs operator interviews. Owner's lost-sales review also points to a cheap safeguard: an agent that watches order volume and search traffic for 30 days after a migration and flags drops.

### How The Pass capitalizes

This is where The Pass's cross-product data matters most. POS-bound tools know past checks. They do not know that Thursday is 40% booked. The Reservations Agent already targets lapsed regulars against quiet shifts, with consent checked in code, quiet hours enforced, and owner approval. **Quiet-night fill from reservation data** should be the flagship marketing proof.

Four additions turn the category's main complaints into features:

1. **A holdout group on every campaign by default,** reporting incremental covers and revenue in cents. This answers the "40% measure no ROI" complaint directly.
2. **Recognition-first loyalty:** visit-based VIP tiers, a "regular" flag at the host stand, and a monthly reward-cost cap enforced by the policy check.
3. **Done-for-you 10DLC registration** under a Twilio ISV setup.

Gift cards are not started. They are a self-contained Stripe build and should default to no expiry and no fees, with per-state liability reporting.

## Reviews, listings and ads all lack the one number owners trust

### Reviews: drafting is free, context is not

Reputation tools fall into three price bands:

| Band | Vendors | Price | Terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enterprise suites | Birdeye | **$299 / $399 / $449 per location per month**, prepaid annual ([Birdeye](https://birdeye.com/blog/what-does-birdeye-cost)) | 12-month auto-renewing contracts |
| Enterprise suites | Podium | ~$399–$599+ | **62–65 BBB complaints in three years**, mostly billing and auto-renewal ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/us/ut/lehi/profile/computer-software-developers/podium-1166-90023083/complaints)) |
| Restaurant specialists | Marqii | Base $90, Growth $145, Scale $180 (adds AI-suggested replies), $250 package, all annual ([Marqii pricing](https://www.marqii.com/pricing)) | Annual contracts |
| POS-bundled | Toast Reviews Agent | Requires Toast IQ Grow | See below |

Toast's agent is the closest match to The Pass's approval model: "Nothing posts to Google until you select Approve & send." It is Google only, shows 30 days of history and has no brand-voice controls yet ([Toast FAQ](https://support.toasttab.com/en/article/Toast-Reviews-Agent-FAQ)). Google itself is testing **native AI review replies inside Business Profile** ([SE Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-reply-to-reviews-with-al-41095.html)). That makes plain drafting a free commodity.

The quieter problem is that Google deletes replies without telling the business. In a study of 12,752 rejected replies, **92.6% answered 5-star reviews with templated language**, and the rejected replies were posted an average of about 50 days after the review ([SE Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/rejected-google-review-replies-analysis-41457.html)). The FTC's Consumer Review Rule bans review gating and sentiment-conditioned incentives, and the FTC sent warning letters in early 2026 ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/consumer-products-and-retail-navigator/2026/01/ftc-warning-letters-over-consumer-review-rule)).

The Pass's Reviews Agent (draft, approve, post to Google) is already built. It cannot win on drafting. It can win on booking context that no one else has, for example: "your 7:30 table seated at 7:55." It can also win by enforcing reply hygiene in code:

- expire or escalate drafts older than seven days
- block near-duplicate replies before posting
- let owners pre-approve short replies to 4–5 star reviews
- log in `change_log` that review requests went to every seated guest, as proof of FTC compliance

### Listings: the pain is Google overriding the owner

Listing sync is commodity-priced. Moz Local runs about $16–$40 per location per month ([CostBench](https://costbench.com/software/seo-tools/moz-local/)), Yext about $199–$999 per location per year ([SocialPilot](https://www.socialpilot.co/?p=712068)), and Marqii Base $90 with POS menu sync.

The real complaint is drift. Anyone can suggest edits, and the feature cannot be disabled ([Synup](https://synup.com/how-to/prevent-suggested-edits-unauthorized-changes-on-your-google-business-profile/)). In one forum report, a Google robocall changed a listing's hours after a staffer misspoke ([LocalSearchForum](https://localsearchforum.com/threads/im-getting-calls-from-google-maps-at-650-206-5555-asking-about-clients-hours-are-they-legit.57546/)). Hijacking a listing's phone number "is (still) easy" ([Near Media](https://nearmedia.co/p/2c516c6d-138d-4f52-b385-5b8996007088)).

The Pass already pushes hours from its own service periods, which is the source of truth listing tools lack. A **listing-drift guard** is cheap to build and protects reservation traffic directly. It would re-read GBP hours, phone, website and booking URL on a schedule, then revert changes or alert the owner. Holiday hours can be generated from reservation-system closures.

### Ads: no one ties spend to seated covers

Restaurants buy ads through three channels:

| Channel | How it's priced | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Agencies | **10–20% of media spend**, often with a $500+ monthly minimum | The model rewards higher spend regardless of results ([Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/restaurant-marketing-agency); [ALM Corp](https://almcorp.com/blog/google-ads-management-pricing-2026/)) |
| Platform add-ons | Popmenu's done-for-you "Boost & paid ads" | Priced on top of the base plan ([Popmenu pricing](https://get.popmenu.com/pricing)) |
| Marketplace ads | DoorDash Sponsored Listings, cost per order ([DoorDash](https://help.doordash.com/en-ca/advertising/article/overview-sponsored-listings)) | Competes for the same marketing dollars |

The research found no Owner.com or Toast product for Google or Meta ads. Agencies blame wasted Meta spend on boosted posts, no offer and no tracking ([86 Empty Seats](https://86emptyseats.com/blog/restaurant-facebook-ads/)). Google added Performance Max channel reporting in 2025, but advertisers still cannot shift budget between channels or turn channels off ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-begins-rolling-out-performance-max-channel-performance-reporting-456251)).

This is The Pass's most defensible capability. Its **Paid Ads and Search Terms agents run on real Google Ads and Meta data, with Google writes (budget, pause, negative keyword) on approval**. Meta is currently read-only, per `docs/AGENTIC-PLATFORM.md`. The next step no competitor offers independents is **closed-loop attribution**: send seated covers and their value back to Google Ads and Meta as offline conversions, then report cost per seated cover and cost per new guest. Missing data shows "—", never $0. A second step uses capacity: pause or lower bids when the book is full or the restaurant is closed.

The pricing comparison is simple. An agency charging 15% of $3,000 a month in spend costs **$450 a month** before minimums. The Pass's agent proposes changes inside hard spending caps for a flat fee, with no incentive to raise spend.

### Incumbents' trust problem is The Pass's positioning

Three weaknesses run across the leaders.

**The FTC lawsuit against Toast.** On October 29, 2026, the FTC, seven states, LA County and San Francisco sued Toast. The alleged practices include:

- deceptive "$0" Starter Kits
- a $150 early termination fee per remaining month, buried in a 25-page merchant agreement
- add-ons billed without consent
- cancellation only by phone, through a weekday-only "churn line"
- Toast Capital loans that default when a borrower switches POS

Toast disputes the claims ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/ftc-and-seven-states-sue-toast)). One merchant was charged **$5,318.87** to leave ([Quartz](https://qz.com/ftc-states-sue-toast-hidden-fees-subscriptions-102926)).

**DoorDash and guest data.** SevenRooms says first-party data does not flow to the DoorDash marketplace ([SevenRooms](https://sevenrooms.com/blog/guest-data-sevenrooms/)). A DoorDash executive called merging delivery and in-store data "still to be determined" ([Restaurant Business](https://restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/sevenrooms-deal-doordash-enters-dining-room)).

**Paid agents with low adoption.** AI is sold as a paid upsell, and fewer than a quarter of Toast locations used Toast IQ weekly.

The Pass's architecture is the counter to all three. Every action goes into the change log with its actor. Owners approve anything that spends money or messages guests. The restaurant owns its ad accounts and guests. Pricing is flat and month to month. These are product facts, not marketing claims, and they should be written into the contract as well: no exclusivity, no inventory requirements, export any time.

The Pass should integrate with Toast and Square rather than fight them. 97% of multi-unit full-service operators run one POS across all venues ([FSR](https://www.fsrmagazine.com/feature/restaurants-reach-a-technology-turning-point-rooted-in-simplicity/)). Open does the same as a Toast partner ([Toast directory](https://pos.toasttab.com/partners/directory/Open)).

### Market size

Technomic counted **412,498 independent restaurants at the end of 2025**, down from 422,001 (−2.3%). Full-service independents lost a net 6,400 units. Chain units rose 1.4% to more than 263,000 ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/operations/number-independent-restaurants-declined-23-2025)). Broader foodservice counts reach 860,000+ ([ScrapeHero](https://www.scrapehero.com/fast-food-and-restaurant-closures-in-us-store-closure-report)). The NRA projects **$1.55T** in 2026 industry sales ([NRA](https://restaurant.org/research-and-media/media/press-releases/persistent-cost-increases-and-enduring-demand-will-shape-the-restaurant-industry-in-2026/)).

At a flat $499 to about $1,000 per location per month, the independent base implies a **dollar TAM of roughly $2.5–5B ARR**. That is an inference from those counts.

The serviceable segment is full-service independents and small groups, where reservations, deposits and guest CRM matter. If about half of independents are full-service, that segment is around 200,000 locations, but no sourced split exists, so this is an assumption to validate.

Two structural facts shape the plan. First, net closures of about 2.3% a year set a floor under logo churn. Second, operators are consolidating vendors. Multi-unit operators average 11.4 tech vendors, down from 13.8, and 54% are actively consolidating ([FSRI](https://foodserviceresearchinstitute.com/blog/restaurant-technology-trends)). Buyers of best-of-breed point solutions fell from 31% to 24% ([Transact](https://www.transact-tech.com/resources/blog/restaurant-tech-stack-consolidation)). Both surveys are vendor-adjacent.

No credible figure was found for tech spend per independent location.

### The prioritized wedge

**First, lead with a free leak audit, Owner's proven funnel adapted to dine-in.** Owner moved from door-knocking and cold email to a free Grader that does the work before the prospect pays. The Pass's version would check four things:

- the restaurant's Google profile for drift and a missing Reserve button
- its website
- review-reply health against Google's rejection patterns
- what the current reservation stack costs, including per-cover fees and the 2% cut of deposits

It would then generate a working site with a booking page. This uses agents The Pass already has and fits founder-led network selling.

**Second, sell a reservations-led core plan near the POS-native band.** At or below about $199 per location, it would include booking page, host stand, targeted deposits at processing cost, waitlist and notify list, guest CRM, import with notes preserved, and export. Toast Tables Plus at about $199 and Yelp at about $129 set the band. No independent, flat-priced, restaurant-owned option has momentum at that price.

**Third, run switch kits on a calendar.** Each kit gets its own landing page and importer:

| Target | Why now |
|---|---|
| Tock venues | Forced migration to Resy |
| OpenTable restaurants | Approaching spring-2027 anniversaries of the March 2026 terms, with outreach 60–90 days ahead, plus coexistence mode until the contract ends |
| SevenRooms customers | Uneasy about DoorDash and guest data |
| Toast merchants | Following the FTC case |
| Caterers | Facing Uber's ezCater commissions |

**Fourth, upsell a flat full-suite plan, likely $499–$799 per location.** It would add the Reservations Agent's quiet-night fill with holdout-measured lift, booking-aware reviews, listing-drift guard, gift cards, catering and cover-attributed ads. It would be positioned against the roughly $1,500–$2,500 stack, and voice answering bundled in would be a strong price anchor.

The order of work follows the gaps: live Stripe deposits, import of notes and history, host-stand offline mode and Reserve with Google certification first. Ordering modifiers and POS hand-off come later, because ordering is where Owner and Open are strongest.

The research leaves real uncertainty. It found **no willingness-to-pay data for a flat all-in-one at independent scale**, no public CAC or churn benchmarks for restaurant SMB SaaS beyond Owner's 4.5:1 LTV:CAC in 2024 ([GetLatka](https://getlatka.com/companies/owner.com/interviews/owner-com-kyle-norton-2024)), and no Reddit or first-person operator threads. The price points above are hypotheses for 20–30 discovery interviews, not conclusions.

## Conclusion

The competitive map has changed in a way that favors The Pass's architecture more than its feature list. Dining-room software used to be a contest over diner networks. In 2026 it became a contest over who the restaurant can trust with its guests: a travel company that sets contract terms, a card network that steers diners, or a delivery marketplace that wants the guest's next order. Agents raise the stakes. Software that acts on its own needs an approval queue and a change log, and the FTC's case against Toast shows what happens when vendors bill and lock in without consent. The Pass's "agents propose, owners approve" design is therefore a sales argument, not just an engineering choice.

The strategic implication is to avoid fighting Owner on its home ground of ordering and websites, where it has $360M in recent funding and a free-audit engine. The Pass should instead own the record of who actually sat down. That record is the one data set Owner, Open and Toast's POS view lack. It makes reviews specific, marketing measurable against empty tables, and ad spend accountable to seated covers. A buyer of The Pass would be paying for exactly that guest graph.
